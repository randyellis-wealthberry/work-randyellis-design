py-0.5 text-xs">Decision <!-- -->1</span></div><h3 id="key-decisions-decision-1-heading" class="inline-block transition-colors duration-200 hover:text-zinc-600 dark:hover:text-zinc-300 mb-3 text-xl font-semibold">One interface for novices and experts, not two</h3><div class="space-y-3"><p class="text-foreground text-base leading-relaxed font-medium">I designed a single interface with progressive disclosure—advanced controls surfaced as users demonstrated competence—instead of splitting the app into beginner and expert modes.</p><div><p class="text-muted-foreground mb-1 text-xs tracking-wide uppercase">Why, and what I passed on</p><p class="text-muted-foreground text-base leading-relaxed">Two distinct modes is the cleaner UX answer and I passed on it deliberately: it doubles the design and build surface, and it forces a self-identification choice on first run that novices routinely get wrong. Progressive disclosure kept one path through the product at the cost of burying expert features behind demonstrated behavior, which meant experienced gardeners had to earn their way to depth they already knew they wanted.</p></div><div><p class="text-muted-foreground mb-1 text-xs tracking-wide uppercase">What happened</p><p class="text-muted-foreground text-base leading-relaxed">It cut both ways, as designed. Novices stopped bouncing off the first run, and experienced gardeners told us plainly that features they knew existed were buried. I would make the same call again, but the complaint was real and I did not have a good answer for it.</p></div></div></article><article aria-labelledby="key-decisions-decision-2-heading" class="bg-muted/30 hover:bg-muted/50 rounded-lg p-5 transition-colors duration-200 md:p-6"><div class="mb-3 flex flex-wrap items-center gap-2"><span data-slot="badge" class="inline-flex items-center justify-center rounded-md border font-medium w-fit whitespace-nowrap shrink-0 [&amp;&gt;svg]:size-3 gap-1 [&amp;&gt;svg]:pointer-events-none focus-visible:border-ring focus-visible:ring-ring/50 focus-visible:ring-[3px] aria-invalid:ring-destructive/20 dark:aria-invalid:ring-destructive/40 aria-invalid:border-destructive transition-[color,box-shadow] overflow-hidden text-foreground [a&amp;]:hover:bg-accent [a&amp;]:hover:text-accent-foreground px-2 py-0.5 text-xs">Decision <!-- -->2</span></div><h3 id="key-decisions-decision-2-heading" class="inline-block transition-colors duration-200 hover:text-zinc-600 dark:hover:text-zinc-300 mb-3 text-xl font-semibold">Ranked candidates over a single confident answer</h3><div class="space-y-3"><p class="text-foreground text-base leading-relaxed font-medium">When plant recognition returned a match, I showed the top candidates and made the user confirm one, rather than presenting a single identification as fact.</p><div><p class="text-muted-foreground mb-1 text-xs tracking-wide uppercase">Why, and what I passed on</p><p class="text-muted-foreground text-base leading-relaxed">A single auto-answer is the better demo and the better first-run moment. But a wrong identification in a plant-care app doesn&#x27;t just embarrass the product—it sends someone down an incorrect watering and light schedule for a living thing. Putting the model&#x27;s uncertainty in front of the user cost one extra tap on every identification and made confirmation an explicit act, which is what let community verification mean something downstream.</p></div><div><p class="text-muted-foreground mb-1 text-xs tracking-wide uppercase">What happened</p><p class="text-muted-foreground text-base leading-relaxed">The confirmation step turned out to build trust rather than just protect against error — people believed an identification more once they had chosen it themselves than when the app simply asserted one.</p></div></div></article><article aria-labelledby="key-decisions-decision-3-heading" class="bg-muted/30 hover:bg-muted/50 rounded-lg p-5 transition-colors duration-200 md:p-6"><div class="mb-3 -->3</span></div><h3 id="key-decisions-decision-3-heading" class="inline-block transition-colors duration-200 hover:text-zinc-600 dark:hover:text-zinc-300 mb-3 text-xl font-semibold">A rating mechanic designed for volume, not depth</h3><div class="space-y-3"><p class="text-foreground text-base leading-relaxed font-medium">I kept photo rating to a single low-effort tap, so contributing required no writing and almost no thought.</p><div><p class="text-muted-foreground mb-1 text-xs tracking-wide uppercase">Why, and what I passed on</p><p class="text-muted-foreground text-base leading-relaxed">The alternative was a richer review—stars, tags, commentary—which produces better individual data and far less of it. I wanted contribution from the majority of users who would never write a review at all, and accepted shallower signal per interaction to get it.</p></div><div><p class="text-muted-foreground mb-1 text-xs tracking-wide uppercase">What happened</p><p class="text-muted-foreground text-base leading-relaxed">Photo ratings reached 3.4M against 350K uploads—roughly ten ratings for every photo contributed. The ratio is the mechanic working as intended, not an accident of traffic.</p></div></div></article><article aria-labelledby="key-decisions-decision-4-heading" class="bg-muted/30 hover:bg-muted/50 rounded-lg p-5 transition-colors duration-200 md:p-6"><div class="mb-3 flex flex-wrap items-center gap-2"><span data-slot="badge" class="inline-flex items-center justify-center rounded-md border font-medium w-fit whitespace-nowrap shrink-0 [&amp;&gt;svg]:size-3 gap-1 [&amp;&gt;svg]:pointer-events-none focus-visible:border-ring focus-visible:ring-ring/50 focus-visible:ring-[3px] aria-invalid:ring-destructive/20 dark:aria-invalid:ring-destructive/40 aria-invalid:border-destructive transition-[color,box-shadow] overflow-hidden text-foreground [a&amp;]:hover:bg-accent [a&amp;]:hover:text-accent-foreground px-2 py-0.5 text-xs">Decision <!-- -->4</span></div><h3 id="key-decisions-decision-4-heading" class="inline-block transition-colors duration-200 hover:text-zinc-600 dark:hover:text-zinc-300 mb-3 text-xl font-semibold">Widening the radius instead of shipping an empty feed</h3><div class="space-y-3"><p class="text-foreground text-base leading-relaxed font-medium">Location-scoped discovery expanded its radius automatically when local density was too low to fill a feed.</p><div><p class="text-muted-foreground mb-1 text-xs tracking-wide uppercase">Why, and what I passed on</p><p class="text-muted-foreground text-base leading-relaxed">Geographic relevance is the whole point in gardening—climate and plant availability decide whether advice is useful—but strict location scoping punishes exactly the users who need the app most, the ones with no gardening community nearby. Rather than choose between relevance and a populated feed, I let scope degrade gracefully: tight where there was density, wider where there wasn&#x27;t.</p></div><div><p class="text-muted-foreground mb-1 text-xs tracking-wide uppercase">What happened</p><p class="text-muted-foreground text-base leading-relaxed">It did what it was built for: users in sparse regions got a populated feed instead of a blank screen.
